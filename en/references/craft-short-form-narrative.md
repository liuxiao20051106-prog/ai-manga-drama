# Short-Form Narrative and Episode Rhythm

Load when writing a script, restructuring an episode, or diagnosing "the opening loses people", "viewers scroll away mid-episode", or "every episode feels like a log". SKILL.md carries the baseline (every scene has goal, resistance, change, consequence; every ending leaves momentum); this is the full expansion.

> Related: turning rhythm into shots in [Shot Language](craft-shot-language.md); dialogue word budgets in [Dialogue, Voice, and Sound](craft-dialogue-voice-and-sound.md); long-arc design in [Commercialization and Analytics](commercialization-and-analytics.md).

## I. Short form is an attention budget

Viewers do not "watch to the end" — they decide **every second** whether to keep going. Treat an episode as a budget curve:

| Window | What the viewer asks | What you must deliver |
|--------|----------------------|-----------------------|
| 0-3 s | What is this? Worth stopping? | conflict, anomaly, or a strong emotional image — no worldbuilding, no introductions |
| 3-15 s | What is happening? | character + goal + resistance, settled once and clearly |
| 15 s-70% | What next? | a new piece of information or a small turn every 5-10 seconds |
| final 20% | How does it end? | pay off this episode's promise with one clear change |
| last 3 s | And then? | a hook: suspense / reversal / emotional linger / a choice |

**Do not apply the seconds mechanically.** These are reference proportions that shift with genre, platform, and speech rate. The three durable rules are: do not open with setup, do not stall in the middle, do not end clean.

## II. Three episode templates

1. **Suspense first** (best for cold-start vertical feeds)
   Show the anomaly or outcome, rewind to how it happened, then open a bigger question at the end.
   `hook of outcome -> process -> larger question`
2. **Problem and solution** (daily life, healing, comedy)
   One concrete small trouble, attempts and setbacks, resolution through character, then cost or afterglow.
   `trouble -> two failures -> turn -> resolution`
3. **Progressive reveal** (mystery, rule-based stories)
   Clue A, conclusion 1, something is off, clue B overturns it, new conclusion.
   `deduce -> overturn -> deduce again` — every overturn is a small payoff.

## III. One episode delivers one change

A pile of events is not a story. At the end of the episode you must be able to answer: **what went from A to B?**

- Relationship: strangers -> trust; trust -> rupture
- Situation: safe -> hunted; in debt -> cornered
- Knowledge: thought he was dead -> recognizes he is alive
- Emotion: hope -> despair (or the reverse)

An episode that cannot fill in A-to-B should be merged or cut.

## IV. Four hook types

An ending hook is not "unfinished", it is **a reason the viewer cannot skip the next episode**:

| Type | Device | Example |
|------|--------|---------|
| Suspense | delay the outcome | He opens the box, his face changes — cut to black |
| Reversal | overturn what the viewer just believed | The character they trusted removes the mask |
| Emotion | stop at the sharpest point | She finally speaks, and it is the cruellest thing she could say |
| Choice | freeze at the decision | The phone rings twice; his hand hovers over the answer button |

Advanced: **interruption** (the truth is cut off by an external event), **countdown** (a deadline is named, and little time remains at the cut), **anti-cliché** (everyone expects him to back down, and he does not).

**Hooks must arise from the plot.** Deliberately hiding information the viewer already saw reads as an editing error, not as suspense.

## V. Dialogue and information density budget

- Mandarin voice-over commonly runs about **4-5 characters per second** (faster when agitated or comedic). Work backwards to the usable dialogue budget: 30 s is roughly 90-130 characters, 60 s roughly 180-250.
- Over budget means cut. **Cutting lines always beats speeding up delivery** — speed kills emotion.
- The more information the picture carries, the less dialogue you need. First ask whether a single action or prop can deliver it.

## VI. Retention diagnosis

Read the dropout point and work back to the cause:

| Dropout point | Likely cause | Smallest fix |
|---------------|--------------|--------------|
| first 3 s | opens on scenery, explanation, or a title card | open on conflict or an anomalous state |
| 3-15 s | character and goal unclear | one line that states who, what they want, what blocks them |
| middle | flat stretch with no new information | insert a small turn, or compress the whole stretch |
| at 70% | the viewer already guessed the outcome | pay the payoff earlier and spend the ending on a new question |
| the ending | hook too soft, or too resolved | change hook type; end on an unfinished state |

## VII. Serialization: episode closure plus the long question

- **Episode closure**: a new viewer entering at any episode can follow it — the episode has its own problem and resolution.
- **Long question**: three to five season-long mysteries or promises, logged in [the project overview](../templates/manga-project.md), each advanced a little per episode.
- **Promise ledger**: every clue records "planted in episode X -> planned payoff in episode Y". Unpaid setups lose viewers faster than anything else.
- No more than three active subplots; finish one before opening another.
- For seasonal work, the season finale needs a stronger closure and a bigger new question than any single episode (see [Commercialization and Analytics](commercialization-and-analytics.md)).

## VIII. What vertical framing demands

- The frame is narrow, so **characters must sit closer to the camera**; do not spread information horizontally (three people side by side all shrink).
- Top and bottom space is a resource: title or status above, subtitles below, the narrative strip in the middle.
- Lead the eye vertically (looking down, looking up, top-to-bottom action).
- Large-scale action reads bigger in vertical frames; prefer smaller actions at closer framing.

## IX. Common failure modes

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---------|-------|-----|
| Reads like a log | only "and then", never "therefore" | make each action cause the next consequence |
| Flat | no resistance; the character succeeds too easily | add time pressure, cost, or an opponent |
| Confusing | information scattered or too late | settle character, goal, and resistance in the first 15 s |
| Soft ending | the episode ends when the task finishes | end on the consequence or the choice |
| Every episode the same | one template copied mechanically | rotate templates and emotional types |
| Overstuffed | two episodes crammed into one | split the episode; prefer white space over compressed emotion |

## X. Pre-write checklist

- [ ] Does the first 3 seconds carry conflict or strong emotion?
- [ ] Are character, goal, and resistance established within 15 seconds?
- [ ] What is this episode's A-to-B change?
- [ ] Does the middle add information or a turn every 5-10 seconds?
- [ ] Is the dialogue inside the speech-rate budget?
- [ ] Is the ending one of the four hook types, arising naturally from the plot?
- [ ] Did the episode advance at least one long question?
- [ ] Can a new viewer follow this episode on its own?
